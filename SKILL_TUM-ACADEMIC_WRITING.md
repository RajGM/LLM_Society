# TUM Academic Writing

Apply ALL of the following rules when writing, editing, or reviewing academic text. Flag any violations explicitly so the user can see what was changed and why.

## 1. Rhetorical preferences and writer's responsibility

- It is the **writer's responsibility** to ensure the reader understands the text. Never assume the reader will figure it out.
- Organise information in a logical, sensible order. The reader should never feel lost.
- Every text must have a proper **introduction** (containing the central idea), **body** (paragraphs relevant to the central idea), and **conclusion** (tying everything together).
- Present a fair, balanced analysis. Be honest about perspective but never biased or unfair.
- Always cite sources.

## 2. Genre awareness

Identify and respect the genre being written:

- **Essay**: Argues, critiques, or responds to a proposition. Contains a clear thesis statement supported by examples. Audience may not be familiar with the topic.
- **Report**: Objectively investigates, presents, or analyses a situation or case study. Has clearly defined sections, subsections, and headings. Audience is likely familiar with the topic.
- **Thesis**: Presents, explains, and analyses original research. Assumes audience familiarity (but not expertise) with the topic.

The introduction must contain a thesis or purpose statement. The text must have all sections required by the genre. Adjust the level of detail based on the audience's expected familiarity.

## 3. Cohesion and old-new flow

- **Always present familiar (old) information before new information.** This is the old-new flow principle.
- Place the main subjects/themes of the text at the beginning of sentences.
- Use pronouns to connect sentences by referring back to familiar subjects.
- Use transitional phrases (consequently, moreover, however, furthermore) appropriately — but never force them where the logical connection does not exist (no fake cohesion).
- Universal subjects like "we" (for multiple authors), "current research", "this paper" count as familiar information.
- **Topic sentences**: Every paragraph must begin with a topic sentence summarising the paragraph's main idea. A reader should be able to understand the text's gist by reading only the topic sentences.
- **Paragraph structure**: Topic sentence first, then supporting content.
- **Transitions between paragraphs**: Start the next paragraph so that the transition from the previous one feels smooth, using the old-new principle.
- No single-sentence paragraphs (except rare transitional paragraphs).

## 4. Nominalizations — avoid unless justified

Replace nominalised phrases with their verb or adjective forms wherever possible:

- "cause the creation of" → "create"
- "present the argument" → "argue"
- "deliver proof" → "prove"
- "conduct an examination of" → "examine"
- "undertake an investigation" → "investigate"
- "provide a demonstration" → "demonstrate"
- "resourcefulness" → "resourceful"
- "effectiveness" → "effective"
- "validity" → "valid"

**Exceptions** where nominalizations are acceptable:

1. When a short subject referring to the previous sentence is needed (e.g., "These misgivings…").
2. To avoid sentences that are too long (e.g., replacing a relative clause with a nominalised noun).

Eliminate "the fact that" — replace with a noun (e.g., "The fact that GMOs have not been tested thoroughly…" → "Insufficient testing…").

## 5. Eliminating redundancy

- **Repetitive wording**: Combine repeated phrases using relative clauses or back-to-back adjectives.
  - "The most positive responses were given by young interviewees. These young interviewees were highly educated." → "The most positive responses were given by young, highly educated interviewees."
- **Redundant pairs**: Remove words that duplicate meaning.
  - "unexpected surprise" → "surprise"
  - "and also" → "and"
  - "following paragraphs below" → "following paragraphs" OR "paragraphs below"
- **Excessive prepositional phrases**: Reduce chains of prepositions.
  - "Our team of researchers studied a large number of samples of water over a period of three weeks" → "Our research team studied numerous water samples over a three-week period"
- **Redundant modifiers and determiners**: Cut words like "particular," "basically," "some," "kinds of" when they add no meaning.
  - "Any particular future study would basically likely yield some similar kinds of results." → "Any future study would likely yield similar results."
- Never lose or change meaning when eliminating redundancy.

## 6. Active vs. passive voice

**Default to active voice** because it is:

- More reader-friendly (provides a clear agent and action)
- Shorter and more concise
- Free of unnecessary nominalizations
- Demonstrates thorough research by naming who performed an action

When citing research, name the researcher: "Y has questioned the findings of X's study" NOT "The findings of X's study have been questioned."

**Use passive voice only in these four scenarios:**

1. The agent is unknown or irrelevant ("A significant number of obese adults are diagnosed with diabetes.")
2. To state general truths while avoiding conversational "we" ("The problem of obesity needs to be solved soon.")
3. In methodology sections where the reader already knows who conducted the experiment.
4. To maintain cohesion / old-new flow ("Obesity can be prevented. Society, governments as well as individuals must work together…")

**Lacking research is NEVER a valid reason to use passive voice.**

## 7. Avoiding sprawling sentences

- Keep introductory phrases concise — do not delay the subject.
- Use **concrete subjects** and **action verbs** (avoid long, abstract subjects).
- Keep **subject, verb, and object close together** — do not interrupt them with excessive relative clauses.
- Limit relative clauses per sentence — too many is a sign the sentence is overloaded.
- Eliminate redundant modifiers and wording.
- **Read aloud test**: If the writer runs out of breath or trips over the sentence, the reader will too.

## 8. Register and formality

Avoid all of the following in academic writing:

- **Personal pronoun "you"** addressing the reader directly
- **Phrasal verbs**: "point out" → "indicate"; "wrap up" → "conclude"; "use up" → "consume"; "clear up" → "resolve"; "speed up" → "accelerate"; "cut down" → "reduce"; "left out" → "omitted"; "found out" → "discovered"; "hand in" → "submit"
- **Contractions**: "it's" → "it is"; "didn't" → "did not"; "can't" → "cannot" (one word)
- **Starting sentences with conjunctions**: "But" → "However"; "And" → "Additionally/Moreover"; "So" → "Thus/Therefore"; "Also" → "Additionally"
- **Vague/imprecise language**: "a lot of" → "a significant number of"; "more than a dozen" → "approximately 12"
- **Informal/spoken words**: "pretty" → "very/quite"; "hard" → "difficult"; "spot" → "identify/see"; "hotspots" → "habitats/zones"; "kids" → "children"; "broke" → "insolvent"; "big" → use context-specific term; "good" → "positive/effective/beneficial"; "bad" → "adverse/negative"; "small" → use context-specific term
- **Passive with "get"**: "get bitten" → "be bitten"
- **Rhetorical questions** (replace with declarative statements)
- **Emotional words** like "terrible" (use neutral alternatives)
- **Words to eliminate**: "really", "actually", "basically"
- "I believe" and "in my opinion" can weaken objective tone — use sparingly.
- "We" should only be used when there are multiple authors, never to include the audience.
- **Symbols to avoid**: ampersand (&) → write "and"; slash (/) → rewrite; exclamation marks (!) → remove or replace with period.

## 9. Hedging

Never make absolute claims unless the evidence is irrefutable. Unhedged statements sound arrogant and damage credibility.

**Hedging tools:**

- Modal verbs: "can", "may", "might", "could"
- Verbs: "appears to", "seems to", "suggests", "indicates"
- Adjectives/adverbs: "probable", "possible", "possibly", "likely"
- Approximations: "frequently", "rarely", "often", "usually", "occasionally"
- Quantifiers: "a few", "some", "most"
- Conditional clauses: "If integrated properly, AI will…"

**Stance**: The choice of hedge conveys the degree of certainty:

- High certainty: "will certainly"
- Moderate certainty: "is very likely to"
- Tentative: "may"
- Distanced: "allegedly"

Replace absolute language: "undoubtedly the most important" → "one of the most important".

## 10. Avoiding plagiarism

- **Always cite sources** — even when paraphrasing or summarising.
- **Always use quotation marks** when using someone else's exact words.
- **Paraphrasing is a two-step process:**
  1. Replace words with synonyms (but technical vocabulary shared by the field need not change).
  2. Change the sentence structure (convert parts of speech, break long sentences, reorder information, switch active/passive).
- Replacing synonyms alone is NOT sufficient — the sentence structure must also change.
- Include every cited source in the bibliography/references.
- Forgetting a citation is not excusable — it can end an academic career.
- Use in-text citations and quotation marks in notes during research to avoid accidental plagiarism.
- A text should not be a collection of quotes glued together — the writer's own voice and analysis must dominate.

## 11. Punctuation rules

**Hard rules (never break):**

- **No comma splices**: Never separate two independent clauses with just a comma. Use a period or semicolon.
- **No comma between subject and verb** — even if the subject is very long. (Paired commas for inserted non-essential info are fine.)
- **Defining relative clauses** (using "that" or "which"): NO comma before them. ("The paper that laid the groundwork…")
- **Non-defining relative clauses** (using "which" only): ALWAYS use a comma before them. ("Her first paper, which laid the groundwork…")
- **Colons before lists**: Use a colon after an independent clause introducing a list — not a comma, not a semicolon.
- **Quotation punctuation**:
  - No citation → period/comma goes INSIDE quotation marks.
  - With citation → period/comma goes OUTSIDE both quotation marks and citation.
  - Colons, dashes, semicolons always go OUTSIDE quotation marks.
- Use **square brackets** for any changes to quoted text: additions, deletions ([…]), or case changes ([T]wo).

**Stylistic choices (be consistent):**

- **Introductory element commas**: Optional if short and unambiguous; required if ambiguity exists about where the subject begins.
- **Oxford comma**: Either always use it or never use it — be consistent. Use it in long, complex lists for clarity.
- **Coordinating conjunctions** (FANBOYS): Comma before is fine; never use a comma after.
- **Non-essential information**: Paired commas, dashes, or parentheses are all acceptable — choose based on desired emphasis.

**Formatting rules:**

- "Cannot" is one word, not two.
- Use a decimal point, not a comma (3.5, not 3,5).
- Use commas in numbers over 1,000 (every three digits from right: 10,000,000).
- Avoid ampersands (&), slashes, and exclamation marks in academic text.
- Capitalise only proper nouns and titles — no random capitalisation.

## 12. Final checklist before submission

- [ ] Text includes all required formal information (name, title, date, etc.)
- [ ] Formatting follows the required citation style consistently
- [ ] Introduction contains a thesis/purpose statement
- [ ] All genre-required sections are present
- [ ] Paragraphs are properly signalled (tab or double line break)
- [ ] Every paragraph has a topic sentence and is focused
- [ ] No single-sentence paragraphs (except transitional)
- [ ] No sentences begin with "especially", "but", "so", "and", or "also"
- [ ] No contractions
- [ ] "big", "small", "good", "bad" replaced with precise terms
- [ ] No passive voice with "get"
- [ ] "really", "actually", "basically" eliminated
- [ ] "you" and "we" eliminated (unless multiple authors)
- [ ] Correct word forms used (life/lives, proof/prove, safe/save, rise/raise, halve/half)
- [ ] Correct collocations ("make a mistake" not "do a mistake")
- [ ] Correct verb tenses (simple past for completed past actions with time references; no present continuous for facts)
- [ ] Articles not omitted before adjective + singular noun constructions
- [ ] Correct prepositions (demand for, increase in)
- [ ] Correct use of this/these/that/those, less/fewer, many/more, number/amount
- [ ] No comma splices or run-on sentences
- [ ] No commas before defining relative clauses
- [ ] No random capitalisation
- [ ] Consecutive sentences not ending/beginning with the same words unnecessarily
- [ ] "Cannot" written as one word
- [ ] Decimal points (not commas) used
- [ ] Commas in numbers over 1,000
- [ ] All sources cited in-text AND in bibliography
- [ ] All bibliography entries contain complete information per citation style
- [ ] Text read aloud and all stumbling points reformulated

## 13. Writing process

When drafting, follow the **2.5 Draft Method**:

- **Draft 0.5**: Focused free writing — set a timer, write continuously without worrying about quality. Get all ideas on paper in broken sentences and bullets.
- **Draft 1**: Organise and convert Draft 0.5 into coherent sentences. Create an outline to arrange ideas logically.
- **Draft 2**: Refine Draft 1 — fix punctuation, sentence structure, grammar, register, cohesion, and apply all rules above.

## 14. General principles

- **Credibility** is everything: register, tone, hedging, citation, and fair analysis all contribute to how believable and trustworthy the writing sounds.
- **Cut jargon**: Unless a specialised term is widely used in the field, use commonly understood terminology. Jargon often signals the writer's own confusion.
- **Stick to the outline**: Check back regularly. Changes should be conscious decisions, not drift.
- **Read aloud**: If the writer stumbles, the reader will too. Reformulate those sections.
- **Avoid excessive detail**: The audience reads for outcomes and how the writer reached them. Give only as much detail as needed.
- **Academic writing is a conversation**: Write to be read and responded to. Aim for accessible, even lively prose — not lecturing.
- **Be consistent** throughout: capitalisation, punctuation style, formatting.
